## TEXAS CLIMATE OVERVIEW

Texas spans multiple climate zones — one of the most climatically diverse states.
Understanding regional climate is essential for environmental assessment timing,
stormwater design, and flood risk evaluation.

### Climate Zones

East Texas (Piney Woods): Humid subtropical. High rainfall (45-60 inches/year).
  Frequent thunderstorms. Long growing season. High flood frequency.

North Central Texas (DFW Metroplex): Semi-humid subtropical.
  Rainfall 30-40 inches/year. Highly variable — can have drought and flood same year.
  Extreme thunderstorms (hail, tornadoes). Hot summers, mild winters.
  McKinney, TX: ~38 inches annual rainfall, highly variable year-to-year.

Central Texas (Hill Country): Semi-arid to semi-humid transition.
  Rainfall 25-35 inches/year. Flash flood capital of the US — Balcones Escarpment
  forces rapid orographic lifting of Gulf moisture. Edwards Aquifer dependent.

South Texas (Rio Grande Plains): Semi-arid. Rainfall 16-28 inches/year.
  Drought-prone. High evapotranspiration. Brush country.

West Texas (Trans-Pecos): Arid. Rainfall <12 inches/year in some areas.
  Extreme heat, high winds. Desert conditions.

Gulf Coast: Humid subtropical. High rainfall. Hurricane risk.
  Storm surge and flooding primary hazards.
  Most vulnerable Texas coast to sea level rise.

## DROUGHT — ASSESSMENT AND IMPACT

### Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI)
Standard measure of drought intensity.
- PDSI > 0: Wet conditions
- PDSI 0 to -1: Near normal
- PDSI -1 to -2: Mild drought
- PDSI -2 to -3: Moderate drought
- PDSI -3 to -4: Severe drought
- PDSI < -4: Extreme to exceptional drought

### US Drought Monitor
Weekly drought assessment by county. Categories:
D0: Abnormally dry
D1: Moderate drought
D2: Severe drought
D3: Extreme drought
D4: Exceptional drought

Texas drought history:
2011: Record drought — exceptional drought statewide. Most severe in modern record.
  Trinity River near zero flow. Lakes at historic lows. Major agricultural losses.
2022: Significant drought across most of Texas.
  Ongoing drought conditions affecting groundwater recharge.

### Drought Effects on Environmental Assessment

WETLAND ASSESSMENT:
Drought significantly affects wetland expression. During D3-D4 drought:
- Wetland hydrology may be absent — cannot confirm positive hydrology indicator
- Vegetation may show stress — some hydrophytes die back
- Soils may be cracked and dry — hydric indicators harder to find
- Note drought conditions in any delineation report during drought

This is WHY timing matters for wetland delineation in Texas.
Post-drought or during La Nina recovery years — wetlands express better.

GROUNDWATER:
Drought lowers water table — may change contaminant plume behavior.
Recovery from drought raises water table — may mobilize previously
unsaturated contamination (smearing effect in petroleum sites).
LPST sites near lakes or rivers: Lake level changes affect groundwater
and contaminant plume geometry.

SOIL BEHAVIOR:
Drought causes extreme cracking in Vertisol clays (Houston Black, Burleson).
Cracks can be 2-4 inches wide and 3-4 feet deep in extreme drought.
Surface contamination entering cracks bypasses vadose zone completely.
Post-drought rainfall causes rapid swelling — seals cracks but contaminant
already transported to depth.

### La Nina and El Nino — Texas Patterns

La Nina (cool Pacific Ocean): Texas tends drier and warmer.
  Drought risk elevated. 2011 drought was strong La Nina year.
  Reduced hurricane activity in Gulf but can increase.

El Nino (warm Pacific Ocean): Texas tends wetter, especially winter-spring.
  Increased flood risk. Better wetland expression.
  Edwards Aquifer recharges better. Streams run higher.

## PRECIPITATION AND FLOOD RISK

### 100-Year Rainfall Depths — North Texas
Texas experiences extreme rainfall events — design standards based on:
1-hour, 100-year storm (DFW): approximately 4.0-4.5 inches
24-hour, 100-year storm (DFW): approximately 9-11 inches
These values drive stormwater design and FEMA flood zone mapping.

Note: Climate change is increasing precipitation intensity in Texas.
Some evidence that 100-year events are occurring more frequently.
FEMA maps based on historical data may underestimate current risk.

### Flash Flooding — Texas Context
Texas leads the nation in flash flood fatalities.
Primary mechanism: Intense convective storms over impermeable terrain.
Highest risk: Balcones Escarpment (Austin/San Antonio area)
  Where Gulf moisture hits Hill Country limestone — rapid runoff, no absorption.
  Pedernales, Guadalupe, Medina, Llano rivers — flash flood prone.

North Texas flash flood risk:
DFW creeks (Rowlett, White Rock, Elm Fork tributaries) — urbanization
has significantly increased peak flows. Pre-development streams now carry
3-5x the peak flow in 100-year events due to impervious cover.

### Hurricane and Tropical Storm Impact
Gulf Coast: Direct hurricane risk (Harvey 2017 — unprecedented flooding).
North Texas: Tropical moisture from remnants can cause extreme rainfall.
Hurricane Harvey (2017): 60 inches of rain in some Houston locations.
  Demonstrated that mapped 100-year and 500-year flood zones were insufficient.

## TEMPERATURE AND HEAT

Texas temperatures:
DFW: Average July high 95°F, record 113°F (2011).
Extreme heat affects:
- Field work safety protocols (heat illness risk)
- Contaminant volatility (higher temps = higher vapor pressure = more VI risk)
- Biological activity (higher temps = faster biodegradation in some cases)
- Infrastructure stress (pipeline thermal expansion, UST pressure changes)

Winter storms:
February 2021 (Winter Storm Uri): Unprecedented cold. Grid failure statewide.
  Environmental impact: Burst pipes causing releases, facility failures,
  spills and discharges during emergency conditions. Some sites had releases.
  Phase I note: Sites operating during February 2021 may have had uncontrolled
  releases that were not formally reported — check TCEQ complaint database.

## CLIMATE AND ENVIRONMENTAL COMPLIANCE

### Stormwater Compliance — Climate Context
More intense storms = greater stormwater compliance challenges.
SWPPP BMPs designed for standard rainfall may fail in extreme events.
Post-Harvey guidance: TCEQ and EPA expect SWPPP to address larger events.
Document design storm assumptions in SWPPP.

### Air Quality and Heat
Higher temperatures increase ozone formation.
DFW is a non-attainment area for ozone (8-hour NAAQS).
Summer ozone season: May 1 through October 31 in North Texas.
Ozone action days: TCEQ issues alerts when ozone expected to exceed standards.
Construction during ozone action days = heightened air quality scrutiny.

### Carbon and Conservation
Texas has the largest land area of any contiguous state.
Significant carbon sequestration potential in forests, grasslands, wetlands.
Emerging voluntary carbon markets creating value for conservation easements.
Blue Duck Foundation projects: Carbon potential should be assessed alongside
conservation and waterfowl habitat value.

## NOAA CLIMATE DATA SOURCES

NOAA Climate Data Online (CDO):
- Historical daily temperature and precipitation by station
- Requires API token for bulk access
- Station data back to 1800s in some Texas locations
- Use for establishing baseline climate conditions at any site

PRISM Climate Data:
- Gridded climate normals at 800m resolution
- 30-year normals (1991-2020) for precipitation, temperature, vapor pressure deficit
- Best source for site-specific climate baseline
- Access via PRISM Climate Group API

NOAA Atlas 14:
- Precipitation frequency data — rainfall depths by return period
- Official source for design storm depths
- Used in FEMA flood studies and stormwater design
- Texas values updated in Atlas 14 Volume 11 (2018)

## SEASONAL ASSESSMENT CALENDAR — TEXAS

January-February: Winter. Dormant vegetation. Good for site access.
  Best time for geology observation (no vegetation obscuring outcrops).
  Worst for wetland delineation (dormant hydrophytes).
  Watch for late winter flooding as soils saturate.

March-April: Spring. Active plant growth. Wetland hydrology often expressed.
  BEST TIME for wetland delineation in North Texas.
  Golden-cheeked warbler nesting begins March 1 (Hill Country).
  Migratory birds arrive — bird surveys best March-May.
  Watch for late season cold snaps affecting field conditions.

May-June: Late spring. Vegetation fully expressed. Increasing heat.
  Good for Phase I field reconnaissance.
  Ozone season begins May 1.
  Waterfowl depart for breeding grounds.

July-August: Peak summer. Extreme heat. Drought risk high.
  Worst for wetland delineation — hydrology often absent.
  Field work limited by heat — early morning only.
  Soils cracked and dry in Vertisol areas.

September-October: Fall transition. Cooling. Migratory birds return.
  Waterfowl hunting season begins — context for conservation sites.
  Some wetland hydrology returning after summer drought.
  Hurricane risk September-October.

November-December: Fall. Vegetation senescent.
  Good visibility for site reconnaissance (leaves down).
  Waterfowl arriving for winter.
  Wetland hydrology variable — depends on fall rainfall.

## COMMON ERRORS IN CLIMATE INTERPRETATION

1. Not adjusting for drought when assessing wetlands:
   Always note current drought conditions and their effect on findings.

2. Using outdated rainfall depth data: NOAA Atlas 14 (2018) is current for Texas.
   Older design storm values may underestimate actual risk.

3. Missing ozone season context: Air quality issues during May-October in DFW.
   Construction during ozone action days has regulatory implications.

4. Ignoring Winter Storm Uri legacy: February 2021 releases may not be in databases.
   Check TCEQ complaints for sites operating during that event.

5. Missing La Nina/El Nino context: Current ENSO phase affects Texas precipitation.
   Note current phase when assessing drought risk for any site.

6. Missing carbon context: For conservation properties, carbon sequestration value
   is increasingly important to document alongside ecological values.
