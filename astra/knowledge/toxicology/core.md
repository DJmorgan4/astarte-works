## HUMAN HEALTH RISK ASSESSMENT — FRAMEWORK

Four-step EPA process for risk assessment at contaminated sites:

### Step 1: Hazard Identification
What chemicals are present? What are their toxicological properties?
Carcinogens vs. non-carcinogens.
Routes of toxicity: Ingestion, inhalation, dermal contact.
Key sources: EPA IRIS (Integrated Risk Information System), ATSDR ToxProfiles, TCEQ PCLs.

### Step 2: Dose-Response Assessment
Carcinogens (linear no-threshold model):
- Cancer Slope Factor (CSF): excess cancer risk per unit dose (mg/kg/day)⁻¹
- Inhalation Unit Risk (IUR): excess cancer risk per µg/m³ air
- Any exposure theoretically carries some risk

Non-carcinogens (threshold model):
- Reference Dose (RfD): acceptable daily oral intake (mg/kg/day)
- Reference Concentration (RfC): acceptable air concentration (mg/m³)
- Hazard Quotient (HQ): actual dose ÷ RfD — if HQ > 1, concern
- Hazard Index (HI): sum of all HQs — if HI > 1, cumulative concern

### Step 3: Exposure Assessment
Exposure point concentrations: What concentration contacts the receptor?
Exposure factors:
- Body weight: Child 15 kg, Adult 70 kg
- Soil ingestion: Child 200 mg/day, Adult 50 mg/day (residential)
- Water ingestion: 2 L/day adult, 1 L/day child
- Inhalation rate: 20 m³/day adult, 10 m³/day child
- Skin adherence: Child 0.2 mg/cm²/event, Adult 0.07 mg/cm²/event
- Exposure frequency: Residential 350 days/year, Occupational 250 days/year
- Exposure duration: Residential 30 years, Occupational 25 years
- Averaging time: Carcinogens 70 years (lifetime), Non-carcinogens = exposure duration

Exposure pathways (residential = most protective):
1. Incidental soil ingestion: Children primary concern at residential
2. Groundwater ingestion: If potable aquifer used
3. Inhalation of vapor/dust: Volatile organics, particulates
4. Dermal contact with soil: Less important than ingestion typically
5. Vapor intrusion: Volatile organics migrating from subsurface to indoor air
6. Fish/vegetable consumption: Bioaccumulation pathways (mercury, PCBs)

### Step 4: Risk Characterization
Cancer risk acceptable range: 1×10⁻⁶ (1 in a million) to 1×10⁻⁴ (1 in 10,000)
TCEQ acceptable cancer risk target: 1×10⁻⁵ (1 in 100,000) for most pathways
Hazard Index acceptable: HI ≤ 1.0

Risk-based cleanup levels: Back-calculated from acceptable risk targets.
TCEQ publishes Protective Concentration Levels (PCLs) by land use and pathway.
PCLs are essentially pre-calculated risk-based cleanup standards.

## KEY CONTAMINANTS — TOXICOLOGICAL PROFILES

### Benzene
Classification: Group 1 — Known human carcinogen (IARC, EPA)
Primary cancer: Acute myelogenous leukemia (AML)
Secondary: Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, multiple myeloma
Cancer Slope Factor: 0.055 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹
Inhalation Unit Risk: 2.2×10⁻⁶ per µg/m³
MCL (drinking water): 5 µg/L
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 0.06 mg/kg (extremely low — lowest of BTEX)
TCEQ PCL commercial soil: 1.9 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 5 µg/L
Vapor intrusion: HIGH priority — volatile (Hcc = 0.23), readily migrates to indoor air
Henry's Law constant: Significant vapor partitioning from soil/water to air
Key fact: BTEX risk assessment is BENZENE-DRIVEN. Other BTEX components
have much higher cleanup standards. Benzene is the regulatory limiting factor.
At residential sites: Even 0.1 mg/kg soil = potential concern for children.

### Toluene
Classification: Non-carcinogen
Health effect: CNS effects, developmental effects (high dose)
RfD: 0.08 mg/kg/day (oral)
MCL: 1,000 µg/L (1 mg/L)
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 520 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 1,000 µg/L
Relative risk: Much lower than benzene. Often not the limiting contaminant.
Biodegrades readily under aerobic conditions — shorter plume persistence.

### Ethylbenzene
Classification: Possible carcinogen (Group 2B)
Health effect: CNS, liver, kidney effects; possible carcinogen
MCL: 700 µg/L
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 38 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 700 µg/L

### Xylene (total)
Classification: Non-carcinogen
Health effect: CNS effects
MCL: 10,000 µg/L (10 mg/L)
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 490 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 10,000 µg/L
Note: Xylene standards very high — rarely the limiting BTEX constituent.

### MTBE (Methyl tert-Butyl Ether)
Classification: Possible carcinogen (Group 2B); significant taste/odor concern
Primary concern in Texas: TCEQ PCL is 13 µg/L — more stringent than most states
Water quality: Taste threshold 15-45 µg/L — complaints below health threshold
Cancer: Animal evidence of kidney and liver tumors
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 13 µg/L
EPA health advisory (lifetime): 20-40 µg/L (less stringent than TCEQ)
Key: TCEQ PCL more protective than EPA advisory. Use TCEQ standard for Texas sites.
Mobility: MTBE much more mobile and persistent than BTEX.
Plumes: 500-2,000 ft from source. Does not biodegrade readily.
Pre-2006 LPST = always screen for MTBE. Still present in gasoline at reduced levels.

### Tetrachloroethylene / Perchloroethylene (PCE)
Classification: Group 2A — Probable human carcinogen
Primary cancer: Liver cancer, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, bladder cancer
Cancer Slope Factor: 0.002 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹
Inhalation Unit Risk: 2.6×10⁻⁷ per µg/m³
MCL: 5 µg/L
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 0.56 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL commercial soil: 6.4 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 5 µg/L
Vapor intrusion screening level (indoor air): 41 µg/m³
DNAPL: PCE is dense — sinks below water table, extremely difficult to recover.
Biodegradation: Reductive dechlorination → TCE → DCE → vinyl chloride → ethene.
Key: Former dry cleaner = PCE release assumed until proven otherwise.
Phase II for PCE sites MUST include: deep borings to water table, VC analysis,
  sub-slab soil gas, VI assessment for any nearby buildings.

### Trichloroethylene (TCE)
Classification: Group 1 — Known human carcinogen (reclassified 2011)
Primary cancer: Kidney cancer, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, liver cancer
Developmental toxicity: Cardiac defects in fetuses (EPA 2011 assessment)
  This makes TCE near daycare, schools, or residences = VERY HIGH priority
Cancer Slope Factor: 0.013 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹ — oral
Inhalation Unit Risk: 4.1×10⁻⁶ per µg/m³ — more potent by inhalation than ingestion
MCL: 5 µg/L
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 0.37 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL commercial soil: 4.2 mg/kg
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 5 µg/L
TCEQ VI screening level (indoor air): 2.0 µg/m³ (very low — stringent VI standard)
Key: TCE reclassification in 2011 significantly increased risk — older risk assessments
may dramatically underestimate TCE cancer risk. Always use current slope factors.
Developmental cardiac effects: No safe threshold established.
Any TCE site near residential use = flag VI pathway as high priority.

### cis-1,2-Dichloroethylene (cis-1,2-DCE)
Classification: Non-carcinogen
MCL: 70 µg/L
Health effect: Liver effects
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 70 µg/L
Role: Daughter product of TCE dechlorination. Indicates active biodegradation.
Presence: If cis-DCE present, check for vinyl chloride downstream.
Without VC: Dechlorination may be stalling at DCE stage — incomplete degradation.

### Vinyl Chloride (VC)
Classification: Group 1 — Known human carcinogen
Primary cancer: Liver angiosarcoma (rare but distinctive), hepatocellular carcinoma
Cancer Slope Factor: 0.072 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹ — most potent chlorinated solvent
MCL: 2 µg/L — lowest MCL of all chlorinated solvents
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 0.014 mg/kg (extremely low)
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 2 µg/L
TCEQ VI screening level (indoor air): 1.1 µg/m³ (most stringent VI standard)
Key: VC is the END PRODUCT of PCE/TCE dechlorination.
ALWAYS analyze for VC when PCE or TCE is present.
Most mobile chlorinated compound — migrates farthest from source.
VC in indoor air above 1.1 µg/m³ = immediate VI remediation concern.

### Arsenic
Classification: Group 1 — Known human carcinogen
Primary cancer: Skin, bladder, lung, kidney cancer
Cancer Slope Factor: 1.5 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹ — high potency
MCL: 10 µg/L
TCEQ PCL residential soil: 24 mg/kg (background-based for Texas)
TCEQ PCL groundwater: 10 µg/L
Texas background: Naturally elevated in some Texas areas — 5-15 mg/kg typical.
Cannot attribute arsenic to contamination without establishing background.
Sources beyond natural: Pesticides (cotton, orchard history), CCA wood preservative,
  smelting operations, coal combustion ash.
CCA (Chromated Copper Arsenate): Used in pressure-treated lumber until 2004.
  Significant arsenic leaching at old playground equipment, decks, fences.
  Visual inspection of older wood structures at residential = flag for CCA.
Cotton field history: Arsenic-based pesticides used historically in Texas cotton.
  Former cotton fields in development = sample soil for arsenic before assuming background.

### Lead
Classification: Non-carcinogen for cancer, but potent developmental neurotoxin
Health effect: Irreversible neurodevelopmental damage in children — NO safe threshold
  IQ loss, behavioral effects, attention deficits at blood lead levels once considered safe
Residential soil screening value: 400 mg/kg (EPA/ATSDR for play areas)
Residential soil: 1,200 mg/kg (TCEQ for non-play areas)
Industrial soil: 1,500 mg/kg
EPA action level (drinking water): 15 µg/L (action level, not MCL)
Key receptor: Children under 6 are primary concern — hand-to-mouth behavior.
Sources: Former leaded gasoline (pre-1996), lead paint (pre-1978), smelting,
  battery recycling, shooting ranges, industrial operations.
Phase I flags: Former firing range on or adjacent = lead in soil probable.
  Urban fill (pre-1980) = lead paint debris likely.
  Proximity to former smelter = elevated lead and arsenic.
  Old residential structure with soil disturbance = lead paint debris in soil.

### Mercury
Forms: Elemental Hg, inorganic mercury salts, methylmercury (most toxic)
Methylmercury: Neurotoxin, bioaccumulates in fish tissue, fetal development effects
MCL (inorganic): 2 µg/L
Fish tissue advisory trigger: 0.3 mg/kg methylmercury in fish flesh
Texas DSHS fish consumption advisories: Many Texas reservoirs have mercury advisories.
  Check DSHS advisory database before recommending fish consumption from nearby water.
Sources: Coal combustion (atmospheric deposition), chlor-alkali plants,
  thermometer/instrument manufacturing, dental amalgam waste.
Phase I flag: Former chlor-alkali plant or scientific instrument manufacturing =
  potential significant mercury contamination.
Vapor: Elemental mercury volatile — worker inhalation concern at industrial sites.

### PFAS (Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances)
Classification: PFOA Group 1 carcinogen (IARC 2023); PFOS probable carcinogen
Health effects: Liver toxicity, immune suppression, thyroid disruption,
  kidney and testicular cancer (PFOA), developmental effects, high cholesterol
EPA MCLs (finalized April 2024):
  PFOA: 4 ng/L (4 ppt) — among most stringent MCLs ever set
  PFOS: 4 ng/L (4 ppt)
  PFNA: 10 ng/L
  PFHxS: 10 ng/L
  HFPO-DA (GenX): 10 ng/L
  Mixture rule: PFOA + PFOS + PFNA + PFHxS + HFPO-DA ≤ 1 hazard index
Key characteristics:
  Extremely persistent — no natural biodegradation (forever chemicals)
  Highly mobile in groundwater
  Bioaccumulate in blood, liver, and food chain
  Long half-life in human body (3-9 years for PFOA/PFOS)
Primary sources: AFFF (aqueous film-forming foam), food packaging, non-stick coatings,
  industrial processes (fluoropolymer manufacturing), certain firefighting training areas.
Phase I protocol: PFAS beyond ASTM E1527-21 scope — note explicitly.
  Flag AFFF source sites: Military installations, airports, industrial fire training.
  Note as emerging regulatory concern requiring voluntary assessment.
Remediation: Ion exchange (IX) and granular activated carbon (GAC) for groundwater.
  Destruction (thermal, electrochemical) under development — no proven in-situ method.

### PAHs (Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons)
Key carcinogen: Benzo(a)pyrene (BaP) — potent carcinogen, regulatory driver for PAH mixtures
Classification: BaP = Group 1 carcinogen; other PAHs Group 2A or 2B
Cancer Slope Factor BaP: 7.3 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹ — very high potency
TCEQ PCL residential soil (BaP): 0.1 mg/kg
Sources: Coal tar (manufactured gas plants), creosote (railroad ties, utility poles),
  petroleum releases (heavy end), combustion, asphalt.
Former Manufactured Gas Plant (MGP) sites:
  Significant source of PAH contamination in Texas urban areas.
  Former Texas cities with MGP: Austin, Dallas, Fort Worth, Houston, San Antonio, Waco.
  Converted to parks, commercial, and residential — PAH contamination may remain.
  Phase I: Any urban site with pre-1960 industrial use in gas/utility sector = flag for MGP.
Railroad right-of-way: Creosote-treated ties leach PAHs into soil and groundwater.
  Former railroad corridor development = PAH assessment warranted.
Petroleum releases: Heavy fuel oil, diesel, No. 6 fuel oil contain significant PAHs.

### Chromium VI (Hexavalent Chromium, Cr(VI))
Classification: Group 1 — Known human carcinogen (inhalation and ingestion)
Cancer: Lung cancer (inhalation), GI cancers (ingestion)
MCL (total chromium): 100 µg/L — note this is TOTAL, not Cr(VI) specifically
EPA has proposed Cr(VI)-specific MCL (as of 2024 rulemaking)
TCEQ PCL residential soil (total Cr): 450 mg/kg
Key distinction: Cr(VI) vs Cr(III) — vastly different toxicity and mobility.
Cr(VI): Carcinogenic, oxidizing, highly mobile in groundwater. Orange/yellow color.
Cr(III): Low toxicity, relatively immobile, can precipitate from solution.
Speciation required: Total chromium analysis does not distinguish.
  Always request Cr(VI) speciation if chromium detected at industrial sites.
Sources: Chrome plating shops, leather tanning, pigments (chrome yellow),
  industrial cooling towers (anti-corrosion), wood preservatives (CCA).
Phase I flag: Former chrome plating within 0.5 mile = Potential REC.
  Check for yellow/orange soil staining on aerial imagery at industrial sites.

### Nitrate / Nitrite
Not typically a Phase I contaminant but relevant for agricultural sites.
MCL: 10 mg/L as nitrogen (nitrate), 1 mg/L (nitrite)
Health effect: Methemoglobinemia in infants (blue baby syndrome)
Sources: Agricultural fertilizers, septic systems, animal feedlots.
Texas agricultural context: Caliche soils in West Texas with high nitrate.
  Private wells in agricultural areas may exceed MCL without knowledge.
Phase I note: If site in agricultural area with fertilizer history near private wells = flag.

## VAPOR INTRUSION — TOXICOLOGICAL THRESHOLDS

TCEQ indoor air screening levels (VI trigger for investigation):
Benzene:        3.7 µg/m³
PCE:            41 µg/m³
TCE:            2.0 µg/m³ (stringent due to 2011 carcinogen reclassification)
Vinyl Chloride: 1.1 µg/m³ (most stringent — most potent carcinogen)
MTBE:           350 µg/m³

EPA residential air screening levels (more conservative than TCEQ in some cases):
TCE: 0.48 µg/m³ (EPA 2011) vs TCEQ 2.0 µg/m³ — EPA is more protective for TCE
Use more conservative standard when both apply.

VI RISK CONTEXT:
Chlorinated solvents more concerning for VI than petroleum hydrocarbons.
PCE/TCE/VC: High vapor pressure + high toxicity = priority VI assessment.
Benzene: Significant VI concern at petroleum sites, especially shallow groundwater.
MTBE: High vapor pressure but relatively lower toxicity — note but lower priority than benzene.

## ECOLOGICAL RISK ASSESSMENT

Key ecological receptors in Texas:
Aquatic macroinvertebrates: Sensitive to metals, organics, low DO, pH changes.
Fish: Bioaccumulation of mercury, PCBs, PAHs in tissue.
Migratory waterfowl: Lead shot ingestion from wetland sediments, selenium from ag drainage.
Terrestrial wildlife: Soil ingestion by burrowing animals, small mammals.
Listed species: Threatened/endangered = higher ecological scrutiny.

SEDIMENT QUALITY GUIDELINES (aquatic systems):
NOAA ERL (Effects Range-Low): Concentration below which effects rarely observed.
NOAA ERM (Effects Range-Median): Concentration above which effects frequently observed.
PCBs ERL: 22.7 µg/kg; ERM: 180 µg/kg
PAHs (total) ERL: 4,022 µg/kg; ERM: 44,792 µg/kg
Lead ERL: 46.7 mg/kg; ERM: 218 mg/kg
Arsenic ERL: 8.2 mg/kg; ERM: 70 mg/kg

WHEN TO RECOMMEND ECOLOGICAL RISK ASSESSMENT:
Site discharging to 303(d) impaired water body.
Wetland habitat affected by potential contamination plume.
Texas listed species habitat within potential plume path.
Migratory waterfowl habitat with lead, mercury, or selenium concern.
Aquatic habitat downstream of industrial or LPST release.

## RECEPTOR SENSITIVITY HIERARCHY

MOST SENSITIVE (most protective standards apply):
1. Fetuses and pregnant women: TCE cardiac effects, lead, mercury, PFAS
2. Children under 6: Lead, arsenic — highest soil ingestion rate, hand-to-mouth
3. Private groundwater well users: Direct ingestion of contaminated aquifer

MODERATELY SENSITIVE:
4. Residential adults: Soil ingestion, vapor intrusion, groundwater
5. Outdoor workers with soil contact: Dermal, incidental ingestion

LEAST SENSITIVE (for most pathways):
6. Commercial/industrial workers: Limited soil contact, shorter exposure duration

KEY IMPLICATION:
Residential cleanup standards are protective of children — the most sensitive receptor.
Commercial/industrial standards are NOT protective for residential use.
Mixed-use development planned on industrial-remediated site = reassess to residential standards.
Day care or school planned = apply most protective residential standards.
TCE near any occupied building = VI assessment mandatory.

## CONTAMINANT INTERACTION EFFECTS

Mixtures of contaminants may have additive or synergistic effects.
Hazard Index (HI) accounts for additivity for non-carcinogens.
Cancer risks are summed across carcinogens.

COMMON MIXTURES AT TEXAS SITES:
BTEX + MTBE: Standard LPST release mixture. Benzene drives risk.
PCE + TCE + DCE + VC: Dry cleaner dechlorination train. VC drives risk.
PAHs + BTEX: Former MGP or heavy petroleum release. BaP and benzene drive risk.
Lead + Arsenic: Former smelter, mining, pesticide sites. Both require independent assessment.
PFAS mixture: New EPA mixture rule — sum of hazard indices ≤ 1.

## COMMON ERRORS IN TOXICOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT

1. Missing child receptor at residential sites — default to adult exposure
   factors dramatically underestimates risk for benzene, lead, arsenic.

2. Underestimating TCE risk post-2011 — TCE reclassified as Group 1 carcinogen
   AND developmental toxin. Cancer slope factor increased. Older risk assessments
   may underestimate TCE risk by 10x or more.

3. Not checking for vinyl chloride when PCE/TCE present — VC is most potent
   chlorinated solvent and always a downstream degradation product.

4. Ignoring Texas background arsenic — some Texas soils naturally have
   15-20 mg/kg arsenic. Cannot attribute to contamination without background sampling.

5. Missing CCA wood preservative arsenic — old playground equipment, residential
   decks, railroad ties. Common and overlooked arsenic source.

6. Applying commercial cleanup standards to mixed-use or future residential —
   significant risk underestimation. Always verify final land use.

7. Not flagging PFAS sources despite being beyond ASTM scope — 4 ppt MCL
   is now in effect. AFFF sites require proactive disclosure.

8. Missing MGP site indicators — former gas utility operations in Texas cities
   pre-1960 commonly left PAH/coal tar contamination. Urban industrial history
   review must include gas utility operations.

9. Missing bioaccumulation pathway — mercury and PCBs in fish tissue = significant
   human health risk even when water concentrations appear low.

10. Using total chromium cleanup standard for Cr(VI) site — total Cr standard
    (450 mg/kg) is much less protective than Cr(VI)-specific standards.
    Always speciate chromium at industrial sites.

11. Missing MTBE taste/odor threshold — MTBE causes water supply complaints
    at concentrations well below health-based standards. Flag pre-2006 LPST sites
    near drinking water wells regardless of BTEX findings.

12. Not noting TCE developmental cardiac effects — TCE near daycare, school,
    or residential with pregnant women is highest priority VI scenario.
