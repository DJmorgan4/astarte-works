## HUMAN HEALTH RISK ASSESSMENT — FRAMEWORK

Risk assessment determines whether contaminant concentrations pose unacceptable
health risk to humans exposed at a site.

Four-step process:

### Step 1: Hazard Identification
What chemicals are present? What are their toxicological properties?
Carcinogens vs. non-carcinogens.
Routes of toxicity: Ingestion, inhalation, dermal contact.

### Step 2: Dose-Response Assessment
What is the relationship between exposure and health effect?

Carcinogens:
- Cancer Slope Factor (CSF): Risk per unit dose (mg/kg-day)
- Used for: PAHs, benzene, vinyl chloride, arsenic, chromium VI
- Linear no-threshold model: Any exposure carries some risk

Non-carcinogens:
- Reference Dose (RfD): Acceptable daily intake (mg/kg-day)
- Reference Concentration (RfC): Acceptable air concentration (mg/m³)
- Hazard Quotient (HQ): Actual dose / RfD — if >1, concern exists

### Step 3: Exposure Assessment
How much of the chemical do receptors actually contact?

Exposure factors:
- Body weight: Child (15 kg) vs adult (70 kg)
- Ingestion rate: Soil (50-200 mg/day for children), water (2 L/day)
- Inhalation rate: 20 m³/day adult, 10 m³/day child
- Exposure frequency: Days/year at site
- Exposure duration: Years at site
- Averaging time: Carcinogens (70 years lifetime), non-carcinogens (exposure period)

Exposure pathways:
- Incidental soil ingestion: Children in residential settings are primary concern
- Groundwater ingestion: If aquifer is potable and used
- Inhalation of soil vapor/dust: Volatile organics, fugitive dust
- Dermal contact with soil: Less important than ingestion typically
- Vapor intrusion: Volatile organics from subsurface to indoor air

### Step 4: Risk Characterization
Combine exposure and toxicity to calculate risk.

Cancer risk: Acceptable range = 1×10⁻⁶ to 1×10⁻⁴ (1 in a million to 1 in 10,000)
Texas TCEQ acceptable cancer risk: 1×10⁻⁵ (1 in 100,000) for most pathways

Hazard Index (HI): Sum of all HQs for non-carcinogens. If >1, unacceptable risk.

Risk-based cleanup standards: Derived by back-calculating from acceptable risk.
Texas PCLs (Protective Concentration Levels): TCEQ publishes cleanup standards
by land use (residential vs. commercial) and pathway (soil, groundwater, air).

## KEY CONTAMINANTS — TOXICOLOGICAL PROFILES

### Benzene
Type: Carcinogen (Group 1 — known human carcinogen)
Source: LPST releases, petroleum refining, chemical manufacturing
Cancer risk: Leukemia (acute myelogenous leukemia)
Cancer slope factor: 0.055 (mg/kg/day)⁻¹
MCL (water): 5 µg/L
Texas soil PCL (residential): ~0.06 mg/kg (very low)
Key characteristic: MOST IMPORTANT petroleum constituent for risk assessment.
Even small concentrations at receptors = significant cancer risk.

### Tetrachloroethylene (PCE)
Type: Probable carcinogen (Group 2A)
Source: Dry cleaners, metal degreasing
Cancer: Liver cancer, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma
MCL: 5 µg/L
Texas residential soil PCL: ~0.2 mg/kg
Vapor intrusion: High priority — volatile, readily migrates to indoor air

### Trichloroethylene (TCE)
Type: Known carcinogen (Group 1, 2011 reclassification)
Source: Industrial degreasing, dry cleaners
Cancer: Kidney cancer, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, liver cancer
MCL: 5 µg/L
Developmental toxicity: EPA classified as causing heart defects in fetuses
This makes TCE near daycare or residential = VERY HIGH concern
Texas residential soil PCL: ~0.06 mg/kg

### Vinyl Chloride (VC)
Type: Known carcinogen (Group 1)
Source: Daughter product of PCE/TCE biodegradation
Cancer: Liver cancer (angiosarcoma)
MCL: 2 µg/L (lowest of chlorinated solvents)
Key: When PCE/TCE present, assume VC formation in plume
Most mobile chlorinated compound — migrates farthest from source

### Arsenic
Type: Known carcinogen (Group 1)
Source: Natural occurrence in some Texas soils/groundwater, smelting, wood preservative (CCA)
Cancer: Skin, bladder, lung cancer
MCL: 10 µg/L
Texas background: Some counties have naturally elevated arsenic — establish background
Wood preservative CCA (chromated copper arsenate):
  Used in pressure-treated lumber until 2004 for residential use.
  Significant arsenic leaching risk at playground equipment, decks, fences.

### Lead
Type: Non-carcinogen (for most pathways), developmental neurotoxin
Source: Former leaded gasoline, lead paint (pre-1978), smelting, battery recycling
Health effect: Neurodevelopmental impairment in children — no safe threshold
ATSDR MLEF: 400 mg/kg soil in residential (play areas)
Key: Lead risk driven by children — residential with children = most sensitive scenario
Former firing ranges, industrial areas, urban fill: Lead contamination common

### Mercury
Type: Non-carcinogen (elemental and inorganic), methylmercury = neurotoxin
Source: Coal combustion (atmospheric deposition), chlor-alkali plants, thermometers
Fish tissue: Methylmercury bioaccumulates — fish consumption advisories
Texas reservoirs: Many have mercury advisories — check DSHS fish consumption advisories
Vapor: Elemental mercury — dental offices, thermometer manufacturing, chlor-alkali

### PFAS (PFOA, PFOS, and related)
Type: Probable carcinogens (EPA 2016 health advisories)
Source: AFFF firefighting foam, food packaging, non-stick coatings, industrial
MCL (2024): 4 ng/L (ppt) for PFOA and PFOS individually
Health effects: Liver toxicity, immune effects, thyroid disruption, cancer
Key characteristics:
- Extremely persistent — do not biodegrade (forever chemicals)
- Highly mobile in groundwater
- Bioaccumulate in blood and tissue
- At sites with AFFF use: Military installations, airports, fire training areas
Phase I note: PFAS beyond standard ASTM scope — but note AFFF sites

### PAHs (Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons)
Type: Several are known or probable carcinogens
Source: Petroleum releases, coal tar (manufactured gas plants), creosote, combustion
Benzo(a)pyrene: Potent carcinogen — regulatory driver for PAH mixtures
Former manufactured gas plant (MGP) sites: Significant PAH contamination
  Texas had many MGP sites in urban areas — converted to parks, commercial
Railroad right-of-way: Creosote (PAH-rich) used for ties and trestles

### Chromium VI (Hexavalent Chromium)
Type: Known carcinogen (Group 1)
Source: Chrome plating, leather tanning, pigments, cooling towers
Cancer: Lung cancer from inhalation, GI cancers from ingestion
Key: Highly mobile in groundwater (unlike Cr III which is much less toxic)
Former chrome plating shops: Significant Cr VI contamination risk

## ECOLOGICAL RISK ASSESSMENT

Beyond human health, chemicals affect ecological receptors.

Key ecological receptors in Texas:
- Aquatic macroinvertebrates: Sensitive to metals, pesticides, low DO
- Fish: Bioaccumulation of mercury, PCBs, PAHs
- Migratory waterfowl: Lead shot ingestion, selenium from irrigation drainage
- Soil invertebrates: Pesticides, metals

Ecological screening values:
- Sediment quality guidelines (SQG): For aquatic systems
- NOAA sediment benchmarks: Effects range-low (ERL) and effects range-median (ERM)
- EPA ecological soil screening levels (Eco-SSLs)

When to consider ecological risk:
- Site near sensitive aquatic habitat (303(d) water body, TPWD priority)
- Waterfowl habitat affected by potential contamination
- Texas listed species habitat in affected area

## RECEPTOR SENSITIVITY HIERARCHY

For Texas Phase I risk prioritization:

MOST SENSITIVE:
1. Pregnant women + fetuses (TCE cardiac effects, lead neurotoxicity)
2. Children under 6 (lead, arsenic soil ingestion)
3. Private well users (direct ingestion of contaminated groundwater)

MODERATELY SENSITIVE:
4. Residential adults (soil ingestion, vapor intrusion)
5. Outdoor workers (dermal contact, incidental ingestion)

LEAST SENSITIVE (for many pathways):
6. Commercial/industrial workers (limited soil exposure, shorter duration)

This hierarchy is WHY land use matters so much for cleanup standards.
A site cleaned to commercial standards retains residual risk for residential use.

## COMMON ERRORS IN TOXICOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT

1. Missing child receptor: Default to adult without considering child exposure
   at residential sites. Children have different exposure factors and sensitivity.

2. Underestimating TCE risk post-2011: TCE reclassified as Group 1 carcinogen
   and developmental toxin. Risk factors updated — older risk assessments may
   significantly underestimate risk.

3. Missing PFAS at AFFF sites: Not in standard Phase I but 4 ppt MCL now in effect.
   Flag AFFF sites for voluntary PFAS assessment.

4. Ignoring background arsenic: Texas soils have variable natural arsenic.
   Cannot attribute all arsenic to site contamination without background data.

5. Missing bioaccumulation pathway: Mercury and PCBs in fish tissue =
   significant human health risk even when water concentrations are low.

6. Applying commercial standards to mixed-use development: If residential
   use is planned, residential cleanup standards must be met — not commercial.

7. Missing CCA wood preservative: Common in residential and commercial settings
   pre-2004. Significant arsenic leaching at old playground equipment.
