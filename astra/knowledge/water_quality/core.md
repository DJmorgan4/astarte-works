## EPA ATTAINS — WATER BODY ASSESSMENT DATABASE

ATTAINS (Assessment, Total Maximum Daily Load Tracking and Implementation System)
tracks water body condition and impairment listings nationwide.

Key ATTAINS categories:

Category 1: Attaining all designated uses — no evidence of impairment.
Category 2: Attaining some uses but insufficient data for others.
Category 3: Insufficient data to determine attainment.
Category 4a: Impaired — TMDL completed.
Category 4b: Impaired — other control measures will address impairment.
Category 4c: Impaired — impairment not caused by pollutant (e.g., flow alteration).
Category 5: Impaired — TMDL needed (the 303(d) list).

For Phase I: Note any Category 4 or 5 water bodies within 500 feet of site.
Impaired water body + nearby regulated facility = potential connection to assess.

## 303(d) IMPAIRED WATERS — TEXAS

Texas TCEQ 303(d) list identifies water bodies not meeting water quality standards.
Updated every two years as required by Clean Water Act.

Common impairments in Texas:
- Bacteria (E. coli): Most common. Urban runoff, septic systems, livestock.
  Common in: Creeks throughout DFW, Hill Country rivers.
- Dissolved oxygen: Low oxygen from nutrient loading (eutrophication).
  Common in: Trinity River, Sabine River, East Texas lakes.
- Nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus): Agricultural and urban runoff.
  Common in: Brazos River, Guadalupe River, North Texas lakes.
- Mercury: Fish tissue contamination. Atmospheric deposition.
  Common in: Many Texas reservoirs — affects fish consumption advisories.
- PCBs: Fish tissue contamination. Industrial legacy.
  Common in: Trinity River (Dallas), some Gulf Coast estuaries.
- Dioxins: Fish tissue. Industrial legacy.
  Affects: Some Texas rivers near industrial areas.

### Trinity River Water Quality — North Texas Context
Trinity River main stem (Dallas through Liberty):
- Impaired for bacteria, low dissolved oxygen, nutrients
- Significant improvement since 1970s due to wastewater treatment upgrades
- But still impaired — receiving treated effluent from multiple plants
- PCBs in fish tissue — consumption advisories in effect

East Fork Trinity (McKinney/Collin County):
- Bacteria impairment — urban runoff from rapidly developing watershed
- Nutrients from fertilizers and wastewater
- Ecological concern as urbanization continues

## TPDES — TEXAS POLLUTANT DISCHARGE ELIMINATION SYSTEM

TCEQ administers NPDES equivalent under state authority.

### Point Source Permits

Municipal Wastewater (WWTP):
- Permits set effluent limits for BOD, TSS, nutrients, pathogens
- Ammonia limits protect downstream dissolved oxygen
- Major WWTPs near Phase I sites = potential water quality concern
- Discharge point locations searchable in TCEQ database

Industrial Wastewater:
- Industry-specific effluent limits (ELGs — Effluent Limitation Guidelines)
- Monitoring and reporting requirements
- Permit violations = flag in Phase I regulatory review

Construction Stormwater (CGP):
- Required for sites >1 acre
- SWPPP required
- Ceto core competency

Industrial Stormwater (Multi-Sector General Permit — MSGP):
- For industrial facilities with stormwater exposure
- Sector-specific benchmarks
- Annual comprehensive site compliance evaluation

### Nonpoint Source Pollution
Not directly permitted — managed through best management practices.
Major sources in Texas:
- Agricultural runoff (nutrients, pesticides, sediment)
- Urban stormwater (bacteria, metals, hydrocarbons, nutrients)
- Construction stormwater (sediment)
- Septic systems (bacteria, nutrients in unsewered areas)

## TEXAS WATER QUALITY STANDARDS

TCEQ establishes water quality standards for designated uses:

Aquatic life uses:
- Exceptional: Highest quality, minimal disturbance
- High: Moderately disturbed but still supports diverse aquatic life
- Intermediate: Supporting aquatic life but impacted
- Limited: Significantly impacted, limited aquatic life

Contact recreation (swimming, water skiing):
E. coli standard: 126 CFU/100mL (geometric mean) or 394 (single sample)

Non-contact recreation (boating, fishing):
Less stringent than contact recreation.

Drinking water supply:
Most stringent. Applies to public water supply reservoirs and rivers.

Public health standards:
Fish consumption advisory triggers based on mercury, PCB, dioxin levels.
Texas Department of State Health Services issues advisories.
Active advisories: Trinity River below Dallas (PCBs), some reservoirs (mercury).

## GROUNDWATER QUALITY — WELLS AND AQUIFERS

Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) regulates groundwater quality
primarily through the PST program and industrial permitting.

Texas Water Development Board (TWDB):
Manages groundwater monitoring and aquifer data.
Groundwater database: Water level and quality data for thousands of wells.

Edwards Aquifer Authority (EAA):
Regulates Edwards Aquifer use and quality in San Antonio area.
Stringent protection requirements for recharge zone.
Any contamination in Edwards recharge zone = immediate EAA notification required.

Drinking water MCLs (Maximum Contaminant Levels):
Same as federal EPA MCLs for public water systems.
Key MCLs relevant to Texas Phase I:
- Benzene: 5 µg/L
- PCE: 5 µg/L
- TCE: 5 µg/L
- Vinyl chloride: 2 µg/L
- Nitrate: 10 mg/L (as N)
- Arsenic: 10 µg/L
- PFOA: 4 ng/L (new 2024 MCL)
- PFOS: 4 ng/L (new 2024 MCL)

## USGS WATER QUALITY PORTAL

USGS Water Quality Portal aggregates monitoring data from multiple agencies.
Key data for Texas environmental assessment:

Stream water quality: USGS and TCEQ monitoring stations.
Parameters: Nutrients, metals, bacteria, physical parameters.
Historical data: Some stations have decades of data.

Groundwater quality: USGS National Water Information System.
Well water chemistry data for Texas aquifers.
Use to establish regional background concentrations.

## SURFACE WATER PROXIMITY — PHASE I IMPLICATIONS

When surface water is within 500 feet of site:

Regulated facility nearby + surface water = assess connection:
- Is there a stormwater conveyance connecting site to water body?
- Is the site in the watershed draining to the water body?
- Does the water body have existing impairments?

Impaired water body + regulated facility:
- Impaired for same pollutant as facility type → higher concern
- Example: Bacteria-impaired creek + active livestock operation → flag
- Example: PCB-impaired river + transformer facility → flag

Clean water body + regulated facility:
- Note proximity but lower concern if no release evidence
- Proximity concern, not REC based on water proximity alone

## PRIVATE WATER SUPPLY WELLS

Many Texas rural properties use private groundwater wells.
These are NOT regulated under SDWA (Safe Drinking Water Act).
No testing required by law — owners responsible for their own testing.

Phase I implications:
- Site near LPST with private well downgradient = HIGH receptor concern
- Private well within 0.5 mile downgradient of release = flag for Phase II
- Private well sampling may be recommended to protect landowner

Common contaminants in Texas private wells:
- Bacteria: Septic system proximity, agricultural areas
- Nitrates: Agricultural areas, septic systems
- Arsenic: Natural background — some Texas formations
- Petroleum: LPST releases
- Chlorinated solvents: Former dry cleaner or industrial releases

## COMMON ERRORS IN WATER QUALITY ASSESSMENT

1. Missing ATTAINS check: 303(d) impaired water bodies rarely checked in
   Phase I. Standard practice should include ATTAINS query for nearby waters.

2. Ignoring fish consumption advisories: PCB and mercury advisories affect
   beneficial use attainment — relevant context for Phase I findings.

3. Missing private well receptor: Rural sites near LPSTs — always assess
   for private well receptors downgradient.

4. Not distinguishing point source from nonpoint: TPDES permit violations
   (point source) are more significant than nonpoint source indicators.

5. Missing TMDL context: When a TMDL is in place, new discharges face
   stricter requirements to meet the waste load allocation.

6. Ignoring Edwards Aquifer in Central Texas: Any water quality issue
   in Edwards recharge zone requires escalation — unique regulatory context.
