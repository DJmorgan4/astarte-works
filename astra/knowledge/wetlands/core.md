## THE THREE-PARAMETER APPROACH

A wetland is defined by THREE parameters — ALL three must be present
during the growing season to make a positive wetland determination.

1. HYDROPHYTIC VEGETATION
Plants adapted to grow in saturated or flooded conditions.
Indicator status categories:
- OBL (Obligate Wetland): Almost always in wetlands (>99%)
- FACW (Facultative Wetland): Usually in wetlands (67-99%)
- FAC (Facultative): Equally in wetlands and uplands (34-66%)
- FACU (Facultative Upland): Usually in uplands (1-33%)
- UPL (Upland): Almost never in wetlands (<1%)
Positive indicator: >50% of dominant species are OBL, FACW, or FAC

2. HYDRIC SOILS
Soils formed under prolonged anaerobic conditions.
Field indicators (Corps 2010 Regional Supplement — Great Plains):
- A1: Histosol (organic soils)
- A2: Histic Epipedon (thick organic surface)
- A3: Black Histic
- A5: Hydrogen Sulfide (rotten egg odor)
- A11: Depleted Below Dark Surface
- A12: Thick Dark Surface
- S1: Sandy Mucky Mineral
- S4: Sandy Gleyed Matrix
- F2: Loamy Gleyed Matrix
- F3: Depleted Matrix (gray matrix with Fe/Mn concentrations)
- F6: Redox Dark Surface
- F7: Depleted Dark Surface
- F8: Redox Depressions
- F13: Umbric Surface (dark surface + reduced matrix)

Key field observation: Gleying (gray/blue-gray matrix) and redoximorphic
features (mottles, concentrations, depletions) are primary field indicators.

3. WETLAND HYDROLOGY
Indicators of surface or near-surface water during growing season.
Primary indicators:
- Visual observation of inundation
- Saturation visible within 12 inches
- Watermarks on vegetation
- Drift lines
- Algal mats
- Iron deposits

Secondary indicators:
- Water-stained leaves
- Oxidized rhizospheres
- Morphologically adapted plant structures

## NWI COWARDIN CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

The National Wetland Inventory uses the Cowardin classification system.
Each NWI polygon is coded with system, subsystem, class, subclass, and modifiers.

### SYSTEMS (first letter of code)

M — Marine: Open ocean and coastal waters
E — Estuarine: Tidal saltwater habitats
R — Riverine: Streams and rivers
L — Lacustrine: Lakes and ponds >8 hectares or >2m deep
P — Palustrine: Marshes, swamps, bogs, wet meadows — MOST COMMON IN TEXAS INLAND

### PALUSTRINE CLASSES (most relevant for Texas Phase I)

PEM — Palustrine Emergent
Herbaceous wetlands. Marshes, wet meadows, seasonal ponds.
Dominant vegetation: grasses, sedges, rushes, cattails.
Texas examples: Prairie potholes, seasonal playas, roadside ditches.
Section 404 status: Jurisdictional if connected to WOTUS (post-Sackett).

PFO — Palustrine Forested
Wooded swamps. Tree-dominated wetlands.
Texas examples: Bottomland hardwood forests along creek corridors.
Trees: Water oak, willow oak, green ash, bald cypress, cottonwood.
Section 404 status: High value — Corps scrutinizes fill permits carefully.
Mitigation ratio: Often 2:1 to 3:1 for fill.

PSS — Palustrine Scrub-Shrub
Shrub-dominated wetlands. Willows, buttonbush, swamp rose.
Texas examples: Shrubby margins of creek systems, disturbed wetlands.
Section 404 status: Jurisdictional, often overlooked in field.

PAB — Palustrine Aquatic Bed
Submerged or floating aquatic vegetation.
Texas examples: Ponds with duckweed, pondweed, water hyacinth.

### LACUSTRINE CLASSES

L1UBH — Lacustrine Limnetic Unconsolidated Bottom Permanently Flooded
Open water lakes. Reservoirs. Not typically the concern in Phase I.

### WATER REGIME MODIFIERS (letter after class)

A — Temporarily Flooded: Surface water present <12.5% of growing season
B — Saturated: Saturated to surface but rarely flooded
C — Seasonally Flooded: Flooded for extended periods in growing season
F — Semipermanently Flooded: Flooded most of growing season
H — Permanently Flooded: Flooded throughout year
R — Artificially Flooded: Flooding controlled by humans
S — Seasonally Saturated: Saturated but not flooded most of season
V — Intermittently Exposed: Water present except during extreme drought

High-risk water regimes for Section 404: C, F, H, S (persistent water)
Lower concern: A (temporary flooding only)

### SPECIAL MODIFIERS

d — Diked or Impounded
f — Farmed (prior converted cropland — may be exempt from Section 404)
h — Partly Drained
r — Artificially Flooded
x — Excavated

IMPORTANT — Prior Converted Cropland (PCC):
Wetlands converted to agricultural use before December 23, 1985 may be
exempt from Section 404 permit requirements under the Swampbuster provision.
This is a significant exception in Texas agricultural areas.
PCC status requires USDA NRCS determination — not automatic.

## SECTION 404 JURISDICTION — POST-SACKETT (2023)

The 2023 Sackett v. EPA decision fundamentally changed Clean Water Act jurisdiction.

CURRENTLY JURISDICTIONAL:
1. Traditional navigable waters (large rivers, lakes used in commerce)
2. Relatively permanent waters — perennial streams and rivers
3. Wetlands with a continuous surface connection to relatively permanent waters
   (meaning they are indistinguishable from or directly abutting the water body)

CURRENTLY NOT JURISDICTIONAL (generally):
1. Wetlands separated from navigable waters by a berm, road, or other feature
2. Wetlands adjacent to intermittent or ephemeral streams
3. Isolated wetlands with no surface connection
4. Ephemeral streams (flow only after rain)

PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS FOR TEXAS:
- Many Texas playa lakes (isolated, no surface connection) may not be jurisdictional
- Wetlands along intermittent creek tributaries face jurisdictional uncertainty
- Bottomland hardwood forests along perennial rivers remain jurisdictional
- Always recommend formal Corps jurisdictional determination for any permit application

STATE JURISDICTION — TEXAS:
Texas does NOT have a state wetland protection program equivalent to Section 404.
Only federal Section 404 applies. This means post-Sackett deregulation has
a larger impact in Texas than in states with their own wetland programs.

## SECTION 404 PERMIT TYPES

### Nationwide Permits (NWPs)
Pre-authorized permits for minor impacts with minimal environmental effects.
Key NWPs for Texas development:

NWP 12: Utility line activities. Pipelines, cables, utility corridors.
NWP 14: Linear transportation projects. Roads, bridges, culverts.
NWP 18: Minor discharges. <0.1 acre impact, <25 cubic yards fill.
NWP 29: Residential developments. <0.5 acre impact, <300 linear feet of stream.
NWP 39: Commercial/institutional developments. <0.5 acre, <300 linear feet.
NWP 40: Agricultural activities.
NWP 51: Land-based renewable energy.

General conditions apply to ALL NWPs:
- Impacts must be minimized and avoided where practicable
- Compensatory mitigation required if >0.1 acre impact
- Pre-construction notification (PCN) required for many NWPs

### Individual Permits (IPs)
Required for impacts exceeding NWP thresholds or in sensitive areas.
Full public interest review. Can take 6-18+ months.
Requires alternatives analysis — must demonstrate no practicable alternatives.

### Jurisdictional Determinations (JDs)
Approved JD: Formal determination of WOTUS on a site. 5-year validity.
Preliminary JD: Assumes all features are jurisdictional. 5-year validity.
Always recommend approved JD before committing to development plan.

## MITIGATION SEQUENCING

Section 404 requires mitigation sequencing — in this order:
1. AVOID: Design project to avoid wetland impacts entirely
2. MINIMIZE: Reduce the impact as much as practicable
3. COMPENSATE: Mitigate unavoidable impacts through compensatory mitigation

Compensatory mitigation options:
- Mitigation bank: Purchase credits from approved bank (preferred by Corps)
- In-lieu fee program: Pay into a regional fund
- Permittee-responsible mitigation: Restore or create wetlands on-site

Texas mitigation banks: Multiple approved banks in DFW, Houston, Austin areas.
Typical credit costs: $20,000-$80,000 per credit depending on wetland type.
Credit ratios: 1:1 to 3:1 depending on impact and wetland type.

## TEXAS WETLAND ECOLOGY

### Bottomland Hardwood Forests
Found along Trinity, Brazos, Colorado, and Sabine river corridors.
High ecological value — nesting habitat, flood storage, water quality.
Dominant trees: Water oak, willow oak, Nuttall oak, green ash, cottonwood, hackberry.
Understory: Yaupon, American beautyberry, Virginia wildrye.
Indicator species: Bald cypress (strongly hydrophytic).
Section 404 scrutiny: HIGH — Corps protective of bottomland hardwoods.
Mitigation ratio: Often 2:1 to 3:1.

### Prairie Potholes and Playas
Shallow seasonal wetlands in North Texas prairies.
Critically important for Central Flyway waterfowl — ducks, geese, shorebirds.
Often isolated — post-Sackett jurisdictional status uncertain.
Vegetation: Smartweed, spikerush, maidencane, barnyard grass.
Soils: Heavy clay, often Houston Black or Burleson — gleyed matrix.
Conservation value: HIGH for waterfowl and shorebirds.

### Coastal Marshes
Galveston Bay, Sabine Lake, Corpus Christi Bay areas.
Saltmarsh (Spartina alterniflora dominant) and brackish marsh.
Extremely high ecological value — nursery habitat, storm protection.
Section 404 scrutiny: VERY HIGH in coastal areas.
Often also subject to Section 10 (Rivers and Harbors Act) jurisdiction.

### Riparian Corridors
Narrow wetland strips along creeks and rivers.
Common throughout North Texas along East Fork Trinity, Rowlett Creek, etc.
Often PSS (scrub-shrub) or PFO (forested) classification.
Post-Sackett: Jurisdictional if along perennial stream.

## DELINEATION METHODOLOGY

The Corps 1987 Manual and Regional Supplements govern delineation.
Texas uses: Great Plains Regional Supplement (most of Texas)
           Atlantic and Gulf Coastal Plain Supplement (East Texas and coast)

Standard method applies in normal conditions.
Atypical situations require problem area procedures:
- Drought conditions: hydrology indicators may be absent
- Recent disturbance: vegetation and hydrology altered
- Frozen conditions: not applicable in Texas typically
- Farmed wetlands: vegetation converted

Data forms required:
- Routine determination: 1 data point per wetland/upland
- Comprehensive determination: Multiple transects and data points

Timing matters for Texas delineation:
- Best timing: Late winter through spring (Feb-April) — growing season active,
  hydrology often expressed, soils saturated or recently saturated
- Worst timing: August-September — drought stress, dry soils, reduced hydrology
  expression, vegetation stressed

## PHASE I WETLAND ASSESSMENT — REMOTE APPROACH

For Phase I ESA screening (no field visit):

Step 1: NWI query within 500m of site coordinates
  → Identify polygons on or adjacent to site
  → Note Cowardin codes and water regimes
  → Assess high-risk codes: PFO, PSS, PEM with C/F/H/S water regimes

Step 2: SSURGO hydric soil check
  → Hydric percent >25% in site map unit = elevated wetland probability
  → Drainage class: poorly or very poorly = assess carefully
  → Flooding frequency: frequent or occasional = assess carefully

Step 3: Topographic position
  → DEM analysis: site in low area, tributary, or floodplain position
  → Upstream watershed — is there sufficient drainage area to sustain wetland hydrology?

Step 4: Aerial photo review
  → Dark, moist-appearing areas in non-drought years
  → Vegetation texture changes (smooth grass vs rough shrubby areas)
  → Standing water visible in wet season imagery

Step 5: FEMA flood zone correlation
  → Zone AE or X500 with NWI polygon = elevated concern
  → Floodway + NWI = very high probability of wetland conditions

Reporting language for Phase I screening:
CONFIRMED NWI WETLAND: NWI polygon on or adjacent to site. Field delineation
recommended prior to any ground disturbance.
POTENTIAL WETLAND CONDITIONS: Hydric soils + low topographic position + seasonal
flooding indicators. Wetland delineation recommended.
NO WETLAND INDICATORS: No NWI polygons, non-hydric soils, upland position.
Low wetland probability — note data gap if no field verification performed.

## COMMON ERRORS IN WETLAND ASSESSMENT

1. Relying solely on NWI: NWI misses 20-30% of actual wetlands. Not a substitute
   for field delineation.
2. Ignoring post-Sackett jurisdiction: Assuming all NWI wetlands are jurisdictional
   is incorrect post-2023.
3. Missing prior converted cropland: Farmed wetlands may be exempt but require
   NRCS determination.
4. Wrong timing for delineation: August delineation in Texas drought = missed indicators.
5. Missing isolated playas: Playas often not in NWI and may not be jurisdictional
   post-Sackett but still have conservation value.
6. Assuming ditches are not jurisdictional: Irrigation ditches adjacent to wetlands
   can be jurisdictional depending on connection.
7. Not distinguishing PSS from PFO: Scrub-shrub wetlands are often misidentified as
   upland brush. Look for saturated substrate and hydric soil indicators.
8. Missing the growing season timing: Hydrophytic vegetation indicators require
   assessment during the growing season (March-October in Texas).
